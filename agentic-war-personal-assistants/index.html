The Agentic War Is Here. Almost Nobody Has Tried the Weapons.

Something shifted in the last six months.

Chatbots stopped waiting for you to type.

They started working while you sleep.

Google launched Gemini Spark on 19 May 2026.

Manus launched Cue on 28 September.

OpenAI launched ChatGPT Dots the next day.

xAI has Grok Bot.

Meta has Muse.

Five companies.

Five bets on the same idea.

An assistant that does not just answer.

It acts.

Call it the agentic war.

Here is the strange part.

Most people talking about it have never used two of the five.

Cue is invite-only.

Dots sits behind ChatGPT Pro.

So the loudest opinions come from the fewest hands-on hours.

I wanted a clearer picture.

So I put the five side by side, using only what the companies and credible coverage actually say.

No hype.

No winner declared.

Each one has a real market.

1. Grok Bot: the team builder

Grok Bot gives you named Bots, not one assistant.

They share one persistent cloud computer, with a browser and a terminal.

Each Bot gets its own screen.

They connect to your apps, run routines, talk to each other in group chats and hand work off.

Anything risky comes back to you as an approval card.

Purchases need your sign-off.

It is included with SuperGrok, SuperGrok Plus, SuperGrok Heavy, and Cursor Pro, Pro+, Ultra and Teams plans, with its own usage allowance.

It runs on iOS and desktop.

Who it suits: people who want a small crew of specialists, not a single helper.

What it lacks today: a phone number or a wallet of its own.

2. Gemini Tasks (formerly Spark): the one already in your inbox

Google's 24/7 agent still lives inside the Gemini app. The UI now labels it Task (Chat/Task toggle), after folding Spark into the main chat from about 1 Oct on web and 12 Oct on Android.

It works in the background across Workspace and connected apps, including MCP.

It handles tasks, skills and schedules.

It uses your Gmail rather than an address of its own.

It asks before spending money, and Chrome auto browse can shop.

It needs Google AI Pro or Ultra.

It works on mobile, Mac and web.

In Australia, it should work, since Google lists it everywhere Gemini works except the EEA, UK, Switzerland and Nigeria.

Custom MCP apps are US only for now.

Who it suits: anyone whose life already runs on Gmail, Calendar and Docs.

The advantage is gravity.

Your data is already there.

3. Cue by Manus: the one with a phone

Cue is the most radical design of the five.

Each agent gets its own email address, its own phone number, its own card and its own computer workspace.

It can take calls.

It pays with its own card, inside a budget you set, and you confirm payments.

It has memory, automations, group chats with handoffs, and QR "service agents" for places like cafés.

It runs on web, desktop and iOS.

It is free during invite-only early access.

Nobody has published what it will cost after that.

Now the honest part.

Cue has launch coverage, not independent reviews.

I found no independent security audit.

The card issuer is not published.

Liability and refund terms are not published.

Privacy falls under the general Manus policy, with nothing specific on call recordings or data residency.

Manus is reportedly raising at a US$4B valuation after Beijing blocked a planned deal with Meta.

That is a company under real regulatory attention.

Who it suits: mobile-first people with lots of errands.

Calls.

Bookings.

Orders.

If I had one invite, my test would be small.

One throwaway agent.

No connectors.

A tiny spending limit with approval on every payment.

One test call, then check what was stored.

Export and deletion tested before any real account goes near it.

4. Muse by Meta: the one you probably can't use yet

Muse handles email, bookings and purchases across apps.

It runs on iOS, Android and WhatsApp.

That last one matters.

WhatsApp is where a huge share of the world already talks.

There is a free basic tier, with paid plans at US$20 or US$100 a month, per Reuters via ChannelNews.

It is available in the US and Canada.

It is not available in Australia, and there is no announced date.

Most of the coverage I found is third-party, so treat details with care.

Who it suits: people who live in WhatsApp and want an assistant without learning a new app.

5. ChatGPT Dots: the always-on one behind a velvet rope

OpenAI launched Dots on 29 September 2026.

It is an always-on agent running on GPT-6 Astra.

It has its own cloud computer, connected apps, memory and Custom Rules.

Those rules let you allow, approve or block what it does.

That is a thoughtful control model.

It lives inside ChatGPT on mobile, web and desktop, though setup has to start on desktop.

There is one dot at launch.

More dots, and "teams of dots", are planned.

Texting by iMessage or RCS is waitlist-only for Pro users.

The first dot is included with Pro or Business Premium at no extra cost.

Pro is reported at US$100, US$200 or US$500 a month, per Engadget, not OpenAI.

I could not find a clear trial policy either way.

Here is my problem with that.

Not with the product.

With the door.

An always-on agent is a trust decision.

You hand it your apps, your memory, your routines.

The only way to know if it earns that trust is to live with it for a week.

Putting it behind the top tier means most people have to pay first and find out later.

That turns a test into a commitment.

It also means the people forming opinions about Dots are mostly people who already pay for the most expensive plan.

That is a narrow lens on a product meant for everyone.

Who it suits: existing Pro users who want their ChatGPT to keep working when they close the tab.

There is no single winner

That is the point.

Each one is built for a different person.

The builder who wants a team: Grok Bot.

The Google household: Gemini Tasks (formerly Spark).

The errand runner who wants a real phone and card: Cue.

The WhatsApp-first user in North America: Muse.

The ChatGPT power user already on Pro: Dots.

The war is not about one assistant beating the rest.

It is about who gets let in to try.

Invite codes and top-tier paywalls slow that down.

Free tiers and existing plans speed it up.

The assistant people can actually test is the one they will learn to trust.

Access is the real battlefield.

What I'm watching next

What Cue costs after early access.

Whether Dots gets a cheaper door.

When Muse reaches Australia.

Whether anyone publishes a proper independent security review of an agent that holds its own card.

I'll write about each one as the evidence lands.

Try before you trust.
